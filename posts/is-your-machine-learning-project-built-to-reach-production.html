Is Your Machine Learning Project Built to Reach Production?

A set of questions to ask yourself before a model becomes another notebook that never ships.

29 October 2026

The interesting failures in machine learning rarely happen during training. They happen in the gap between a promising result and a system that someone actually relies on.

Signs the project is still a notebook in disguise

  • The accuracy figure came from a clean sample, and nobody has looked hard at how that sample was built or whether it resembles live traffic.
  • There is no agreed owner for the model once it is live. This matters more than people expect, because a model without an owner has no one to notice when it starts slipping.
  • The data pipeline runs on someone's laptop, or as a series of manual steps that only two people understand.
  • No one has written down what the model assumes about the world, or what should happen when those assumptions stop holding.
  • Serving was treated as an afterthought. Batch or real-time, latency targets, versioning and rollback are all decisions that shape the build, not details to settle at the end.
  • The evaluation metric is a leaderboard number rather than something tied to a decision the business actually makes.

Questions worth asking about the data

Machine learning is a data problem long before it becomes a modelling problem. Most of the risk sits here, and it is far cheaper to surface in week one than in month three.

  • Do you have enough labelled examples, and were the labels produced consistently?
  • Is there any chance of leakage between training and test data, where information from the future quietly informs the past?
  • Does everyone agree on what the target variable actually is? Disagreement here tends to surface late and expensively.
  • Could the data carry bias that would make the model unfair or useless for part of your users?
  • If the data lives in-region for compliance reasons, does the whole pipeline respect that, or only the training step?
  • Is there a baseline you can understand, so you know whether the model is genuinely adding something?

Signs nobody will notice when it degrades

A deployed model drifts. Fraud patterns shift, user behaviour shifts, catalogues change, and the world moves away from whatever the training data captured.

  • There is no monitoring against live outcomes, only against the training metrics that looked good months ago.
  • Performance decay would be discovered by a customer rather than by a dashboard.
  • There is no alerting, so a slow decline goes unseen until it becomes a complaint.
  • Retraining is undefined. Nobody knows how often it should happen, what triggers it, or who signs it off.
  • The documentation describes how the model was built but not how it fails, which is the part the next engineer needs most.

What a good result looks like is unglamorous: a model benchmarked against a baseline on a holdout set that reflects reality, wrapped in a scheduled pipeline, deployed with rollback available, monitored against live outcomes, and documented well enough that your own team can take it over. The model itself is one component among several.
