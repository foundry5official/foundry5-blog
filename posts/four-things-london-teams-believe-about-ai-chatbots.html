Four Things London Teams Believe About AI Chatbots

The common assumptions that lead to disappointing chatbots, and what the reality looks like instead.

30 October 2026

Most teams that end up unhappy with a chatbot were not unlucky. They made a reasonable-sounding assumption early on and built everything on top of it. Here are four of the most common, and why each one tends to fall apart once real users arrive.

Myth: A better model will handle the hard questions

It is tempting to think accuracy is a model problem, and that the next release will fix the awkward gaps. It rarely does. A general model has no access to your refund rules, your product catalogue, or the way your support team actually phrases things. It can sound fluent about all of them without being right about any.

The fix is not a bigger model. It is retrieval: giving the system your own documents and data, and making it answer from those rather than from general knowledge. A model with nothing to retrieve from will fill the silence, and it will do so confidently.

Myth: The SaaS widget is basically the same thing, just cheaper

Tools like Intercom and Drift are solid products. But they answer from a help centre your team writes and maintains, and they cannot read a database, check an order status, or reason across a long policy document. The moment a customer asks something specific, the bot hands off to a human. You have paid for the deflection and still absorbed the ticket.

That is fine if all you need is an FAQ deflector. It stops being fine when your questions live in places the widget cannot reach.

Myth: If it is hosted abroad, nobody will notice

Plenty of chatbot tools store conversations outside the UK, train on them, or simply cannot tell you where the data physically sits. For a business handling customer information, that is a compliance question waiting to be asked by someone less relaxed than you are. Being able to describe the data flow, the retention rules and the provider agreements is part of the product, not paperwork bolted on afterwards.

Myth: You can tell whether it works by chatting to it for ten minutes

A demo tells you the bot handles the questions you thought to ask. It says nothing about the ones your customers actually send. Teams often discover the gap after launch, when the bot is confidently wrong in front of real people and nobody can trace where the answer came from.

A good result looks different: answers that point back to a source, clear refusal when something is out of scope, a clean handover when a human should take over, and an accuracy picture built from your own real question set before go-live. That last part is what turns "it seems fine" into something your team and your compliance reviewers can actually sign off on.
