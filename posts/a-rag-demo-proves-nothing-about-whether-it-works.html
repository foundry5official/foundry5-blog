A RAG Demo Proves Nothing About Whether It Works

Why retrieval quality, not the model, decides whether your RAG system survives contact with real questions — and why a polished prototype tells you almost nothing.

28 October 2026

Every RAG project I've seen go wrong went wrong in the same place. Not the model, not the vector database, not the prompt. It went wrong in retrieval, and nobody noticed until real users started asking real questions.

The usual advice is to start small: chunk a folder of documents, embed them, wire up a chat box, and see if it feels useful. That advice sounds sensible and it is quietly terrible, because a prototype built this way is designed to succeed at exactly the thing it cannot measure. With ten documents and five friendly questions, the nearest embedding is usually the right one. With four thousand documents and questions written by people who don't know how your content is organised, it often isn't. The demo doesn't degrade gracefully. It keeps answering, confidently, from the wrong paragraph.

The model is the least interesting part

Teams spend weeks comparing models and days on the pipeline. That ratio is backwards. A strong model reading the wrong passage produces a fluent, well-structured, wrong answer, and the fluency is what makes it dangerous. A weaker model reading the right passage usually gets there. The work that determines whether the system is trustworthy happens before the model sees anything: how documents are parsed, how they're split, what metadata travels with each chunk, and whether search combines meaning with the exact terms your business actually uses. Product codes, statute numbers, surnames and internal acronyms are where pure vector search quietly fails, and where keyword and metadata filters earn their place.

None of this is visible in a demo, which is why demos keep getting approved.

You cannot fix what you never measured

The second thing that goes wrong is the absence of a question set. If you haven't written down the questions your users will actually ask, along with the passages that genuinely answer them, you have no way to know whether retrieval is working. You have only the impression that it is. Building that set is unglamorous and it is the single most useful artefact a RAG project produces, because it turns "the answers feel better now" into something you can argue about with evidence. Precision and recall on a real question set, measured before launch, is what separates a system your compliance team will sign off on from one they'll quietly ban.

There's a data question tangled up in this too. The moment you point a model at internal knowledge, you're handling contracts, customer records, HR files. Where those live, which provider sees them, and whether anyone trains on them stop being engineering details and become questions an auditor will ask. Most quick builds have no answer, and retrofitting one is far more expensive than deciding it upfront.

What good actually looks like

A RAG system that holds up does one thing well: every answer can be traced to a source passage a human can check. Citations aren't a nice touch, they're the mechanism by which trust becomes possible. Around that, you want a repeatable ingestion pipeline so content stays fresh, evaluation numbers you can show, and a clear account of where data goes. It won't feel as impressive in a demo. It will still be right in month six, which is the only test that matters.
